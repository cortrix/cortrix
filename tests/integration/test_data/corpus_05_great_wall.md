# The Great Wall of China

The Great Wall of China is a series of fortifications built across the historical
northern borders of ancient Chinese states. Construction began as early as the
seventh century BC, when individual states built walls to defend their own
territories. After unifying China in 221 BC, Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor,
ordered the connection and extension of these walls.

Most of the wall that survives today was built during the Ming dynasty, between
1368 and 1644. The Ming wall stretches approximately 8,850 kilometers, from
Shanhaiguan on the eastern coast to Jiayuguan in the western desert. Including
all branches and earlier dynasties' walls, an archaeological survey concluded the
entire system spans about 21,196 kilometers.

Contrary to a persistent myth, the Great Wall is not visible from the Moon with
the naked eye. Astronauts have confirmed that even from low Earth orbit, the wall
is barely distinguishable without optical aid because its materials blend into the
surrounding landscape.

Ming-era construction used bricks and stone rather than the rammed earth of earlier
walls. Sticky rice mortar — a mixture of slaked lime and glutinous rice soup — gave
the masonry remarkable strength and durability, and modern materials scientists
have confirmed the amylopectin in the rice contributes to its compact
microstructure.

The wall was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 and attracts more than
ten million visitors annually, with the Badaling section near Beijing being the
most visited.

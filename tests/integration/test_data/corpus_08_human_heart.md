# The Human Heart

The human heart is a muscular organ about the size of a closed fist, weighing
between 250 and 350 grams in adults. It beats around 100,000 times per day,
pumping roughly 7,500 liters of blood through the circulatory system.

The heart has four chambers: two upper atria that receive blood and two lower
ventricles that pump it out. The right side handles deoxygenated blood, sending it
to the lungs through the pulmonary artery, while the left side receives oxygenated
blood from the lungs and pumps it to the body through the aorta. The left
ventricle has the thickest wall because it must generate enough pressure to drive
blood through the entire systemic circulation.

Four valves keep blood flowing in one direction: the tricuspid and mitral valves
between the atria and ventricles, and the pulmonary and aortic valves at the
ventricular outlets. The familiar "lub-dub" heart sounds are produced by these
valves closing.

The heartbeat originates in the sinoatrial node, a cluster of specialized
pacemaker cells in the right atrium that generates electrical impulses
spontaneously. The signal passes through the atrioventricular node, which delays
it briefly so the ventricles fill before contracting.

Coronary arteries supply the heart muscle itself with blood. Blockage of these
arteries causes myocardial infarction, commonly known as a heart attack, which
remains a leading cause of death worldwide despite major advances in prevention
and treatment.

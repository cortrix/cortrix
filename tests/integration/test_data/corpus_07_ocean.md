# The Deep Ocean

The ocean covers about 71 percent of Earth's surface and contains roughly 97
percent of the planet's water. Its average depth is approximately 3,688 meters,
yet the deepest known point — the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench — plunges
to about 10,935 meters below sea level, deeper than Mount Everest is tall.

The ocean is conventionally divided into depth zones. The sunlit epipelagic zone
extends to 200 meters and hosts nearly all marine photosynthesis. Below it, the
mesopelagic or "twilight" zone reaches 1,000 meters. The bathypelagic zone, in
permanent darkness, extends to 4,000 meters, followed by the abyssopelagic zone,
and finally the hadal zone of the deep trenches.

Hydrothermal vents, first discovered near the Galápagos Rift in 1977, support
ecosystems entirely independent of sunlight. Chemosynthetic bacteria oxidize
hydrogen sulfide from the vent fluid, forming the base of food webs that include
giant tube worms growing up to three meters long.

Only three crewed expeditions had reached the Challenger Deep before 2019: the
bathyscaphe Trieste with Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh in 1960, and film director
James Cameron's solo dive in the Deepsea Challenger in 2012. Victor Vescovo's
repeated dives in 2019 made the descent almost routine.

The great ocean conveyor, or thermohaline circulation, moves water masses around
the globe over a cycle of roughly a thousand years, redistributing heat and
regulating Earth's climate.

# Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is the biological process by which plants, algae, and cyanobacteria
convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. The overall reaction
combines carbon dioxide and water in the presence of light to produce glucose and
oxygen.

The process occurs in chloroplasts, organelles that contain the green pigment
chlorophyll. Chlorophyll absorbs light most strongly in the blue and red portions
of the electromagnetic spectrum, reflecting green light — which is why most plants
appear green to the human eye.

Photosynthesis proceeds in two stages. The light-dependent reactions take place in
the thylakoid membranes, where light energy splits water molecules, releasing
oxygen as a byproduct and producing the energy carriers ATP and NADPH. The
light-independent reactions, known as the Calvin cycle, occur in the stroma and
use ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into three-carbon sugar molecules.

The enzyme RuBisCO, which catalyzes the first major step of carbon fixation in the
Calvin cycle, is considered the most abundant protein on Earth. Despite its
abundance, RuBisCO is a relatively slow and imprecise enzyme, sometimes fixing
oxygen instead of carbon dioxide in a wasteful process called photorespiration.

C4 plants such as maize and sugarcane have evolved a mechanism that concentrates
carbon dioxide around RuBisCO, largely avoiding photorespiration and allowing
efficient photosynthesis in hot, dry climates.

# Coffee: From Bean to Cup

Coffee is brewed from the roasted seeds of plants in the genus Coffea. The two
commercially dominant species are Coffea arabica, which accounts for roughly 60
percent of world production, and Coffea canephora, commonly known as robusta.
Arabica is prized for its smoother, more aromatic cup, while robusta contains
nearly twice the caffeine and produces a stronger, more bitter taste.

According to popular legend, coffee was discovered by an Ethiopian goat herder
named Kaldi, who noticed his goats became unusually energetic after eating red
berries from a certain shrub. Historical evidence places the earliest credible
accounts of coffee drinking in fifteenth-century Yemen, where Sufi monasteries
used it to stay alert during night devotions.

The world's largest coffee producer is Brazil, which has held that position for
over 150 years. Vietnam ranks second, specializing almost entirely in robusta.
Ethiopia, the genetic birthplace of arabica, remains Africa's top producer.

Roasting transforms green coffee beans through the Maillard reaction and
caramelization, developing hundreds of aromatic compounds. Light roasts preserve
more origin character and acidity, while dark roasts emphasize body and
bittersweet flavors.

Espresso is prepared by forcing hot water at around nine bars of pressure through
finely ground coffee in 25 to 30 seconds. The crema, a reddish-brown foam on top
of a well-pulled shot, consists of emulsified oils and carbon dioxide bubbles.
